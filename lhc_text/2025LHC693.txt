Anwaar Hussain, J.    The respondent instituted a suit for 
declaration alongwith permanent injunction against the petitioner 
contending that predecessor-in-interest of the parties, namely, 
Niamat Ali was owner in possession of the suit property, detail 
whereof is given in para No.1 of the plaint of the suit, through 
registered Haqdaran-e-Zamin for the year 1988-89 whereas the 
petitioner illegally and unlawfully, with connivance of officials of 
the Revenue Department, Punjab, got sanctioned a gift/hiba in his 
favour through mutation bearing No.1344 dated 27.02.1999, 
however, said deceased Niamat Ali neither gifted the suit property 
to the petitioner nor he appeared before any Revenue Official for 
the recording of the impugned mutation. The suit was contested by 
way of filing written statement. Issues were framed. After 
recording of the evidence, the suit was decreed, vide judgment and 
decree dated 24.01.2019, which has been upheld by the Appellate 
Court below through judgment dated 13.05.2019.

5. 
Factual matrix of the case highlights that the donor who 
happens to be real father of the parties transferred the suit property 
to the son—petitioner, as he was unhappy with the daughter—
respondent, and the donor wanted to exclude the respondent—
daughter from the inheritance. So even if the gift was genuine, 
whether the same was lawfully made. This is the nub of the matter. 
6. 
Before proceeding further to determine the controversy, it

the petitioner that matter needs to be remanded as there is

7. 
Adverting to the legal question formulated hereinabove, it is 
trite that Islamic law permits individuals to distribute their wealth, 
during their lifetime, through gifts, which is a voluntary transfer of 
the property by one person (the donor) to another (the donee), 
without any consideration. The essential elements of a valid gift 
are, a declaration of gift by the donor; acceptance of the gift by the 
donee; and the delivery of possession. However, such gifts must be 
given in good faith, without the intention to cause harm or 
injustice to the other rightful heirs. Islamic teachings emphasize 
fairness in dealings, particularly among family members. The 
Prophet Muhammad (Sallallahu Alaihi Wasallam) advised against 
favoritism among children as quoted hereinabove. This command 
of the Holy Prophet (Sallallahu Alaihi Wasallam) implies that a 
father should not show undue favoritism in gift-giving, especially 
if it results in an unjust exclusion of the other rightful heirs. The 
Messenger of Allah also said as under:  
  
 وا َلرارا اَلَلاوا َلرَراض اَل 3 
     (No harm should be inflicted or reciprocated)

The above quoted saying of the Holy Prophet (Sallallahu 
Alaihi Wasallam) lays down the fundamental principle of 
preventing harm. Moreover, Islamic law provides for the rights of 
legal heirs and does not permit acts aimed at circumventing the 
fixed shares of inheritance as prescribed in the Holy Book. The 
Quran clearly states as under: 
       ِۚٱ نَیَٱذٱَلُأُ ٱَلَِّح َلُاَلِ ا ِرَكٱذِِ َل َ مُكارََلَََُِٱَلۤاِفَلُهِ ٱ ُایایِصَ َُل” 
Allah (thus) directs you as regards your 
Children's (Inheritance): to the male, a portion 
equal to that of two females” 4 
The above quoted verse from the glorious Quran establishes 
fixed shares for the heirs, ensuring justice and balance. Any act 
aimed at disrupting this balance is deemed impermissible. 
Therefore, if a father gifts his property to his son alone, with the 
sole intention of depriving his daughter of her rightful inheritance, 
the father inflicts harm (
  ض  ر  ار)—both material and moral. Such a 
gift is considered unjust and contrary to the teachings of Islam. 
The rationale is that such transactions contradict the fundamental 
objectives of Shariah, which seeks to uphold justice and equity in 
the family matters. 
8. 
Put otherwise, Islamic Law allows owner of property to gift 
the same out of love and affection, and not out of anger for a third 
party (more particularly if that party is legal heir) permitting the 
donor to abuse of this right to circumvent the obligatory rules of 
inheritance. Therefore, any attempt to use gift/Hiba as a tool to 
deprive lawful heirs is legally and morally impermissible under the 
Islamic Jurisprudence. Hence, the impugned gift/hiba even if was 
made by the donor in favour of the petitioner was not in 
accordance with law.

Had there been any ill-will subsisting with the donor against 
the respondent, the former would not have participated in the 
second marriage ceremony of the respondent. Moreover, it also 
belies logic that having participated in the second marriage of the 
respondent, which depicts the cordial relations between the donor

10. 
Matter can be examined from another angle. Perusal of 
evidentiary resume of the petitioner’s side reveals that the donor’s 
anger towards the respondent (his daughter) motivated him to give 
away his entire property to the petitioner, which indicates that the

donor was under significant distress, stemming from the 
embarrassment caused by the respondent’s first marriage, which 
took place without the consent of the donor/father. This assertion 
of the petitioner, even if accepted, (although the donor admittedly 
participated in the second marriage of the respondent), itself 
exhibits that the donor was under continuous distress lasting for 19 
years. This raises a question as to whether gift offered by a person 
in such a state of continuous distress is valid under Section 16 of 
the Contract Act, 1872 (“the Act”), which reads under: 
“16. “Undueلَinfluence” defined.—(1) A contract is said 
to be induced by “undue influence” where the relations 
subsisting between the parties are such that one of the 
parties is in a position to dominate the will of the other, 
and uses that position to obtain an unfair advantage over 
the other.  
(2) In particular and without prejudice to the generality of 
the foregoing principle, a person is deemed to be in a 
position to dominate the will of another-  
(a)  
where he holds, a real or apparent authority 
over the other, or where he stands in a 
fiduciary relation to the other; or  
(b)  
where he makes a contract with a person 
whose mental capacity is temporarily or 
permanently affected by reason of age, 
illness, or mental or bodily distress.  
(3) Where a person who is in a position to dominate the 
will of another, enters into a contract with him, and the 
transaction appears, on the face of it or on the evidence 
adduced, to be unconscionable, the burden of proving 
that such contract was not induced by undue influence 
shall lie upon the person in a position to dominate the 
will of the other.  
Nothing in this subsection shall affect the provisions of 
section 111 of the Evidence Act, 1872.”  
(Emphasis provided)

Section 16 defines “undue influence” as a circumstance in which 
one party is able to dominate the will of another, either through 
actual authority or by exploiting a fiduciary or other vulnerable 
relationship, including instances where mental or bodily distress 
compromises a person’s capacity for rational decision-making. In 
this case, even if the donor’s anger is accepted as a motivating 
factor, the cumulative analysis of the evidence on record suggests 
that he was unduly influenced by the petitioner, potentially lacking 
the requisite mental capacity to make a fully rational and free 
decision. Consequently, under Section 19-A of the Act, such a 
transaction may be considered voidable and the respondent was 
fully justified in questioning the oral gift, as legal heir of the 
deceased Niamat Ali. 
11. 
Even otherwise, the petitioner failed to prove the oral gift 
mutation, which is witnessed by only one witness, namely, 
Yaseen, who was never produced by the petitioner/defendant in 
order to substantiate the genuineness of the impugned gift 
transaction. There is no evidence on record to establish that the 
said witness was not available. No effort was made on part of the 
petitioner to lead secondary evidence if the said Yaseen was not 
available. In this manner, the best evidence available with the 
petitioner was withheld that brings the case of the petitioner within 
the clutches of illustration (g) to Article 129 of the Qanun-e-
Shahadat Order, 1984, which contemplates that an adverse 
presumption is to be drawn against the party, who withholds the 
best evidence5.  
12. 
Suffice to state that the revisionary power of this Court is 
limited in matters of concurrent findings of the Courts below and 
this Court would, as a settled law, not interfere in the concurrent
